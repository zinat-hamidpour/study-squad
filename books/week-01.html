How to read this case

Don’t try to learn everything. Find your level, master its words, and only then climb.

1
The Scene · band 5.5–6
Name what happened, clearly and correctly. Tested in the Easy word test.
2
The Verdict · band 6.5–7
Talk about punishment and causes with natural collocations. Tested in the Medium word test.
3
The Defence · band 7.5–8
Argue with nuance: idioms, precise terms, hedging. Tested in the Strong word test.

Every model answer comes in three bands

Read the band you are now, then the band above it. Notice exactly what changes: usually one collocation, one reason, one honest hedge. Highlighted words are this week’s vocabulary.

Try first, then read

Record your answer before you open the models. Trying to retrieve a word — even failing — makes it stick far better than reading it twice.

CASE FILE #01 · CRIMESTUDY SQUAD

Fingerprints

Small mistakes that give you away. Examiners notice them.

✗ He did a crime.
✓ He committed a crime.
Crime takes commit.
✗ My phone was robbed.
✓ My phone was stolen. / I was robbed.
Steal a thing; rob a person or place.
✗ There was a robbery in our empty flat.
✓ Our flat was burgled.
Burglary = breaking into a building; robbery involves force against a person.
✗ The police is investigating.
✓ The police are investigating.
Police is always plural.
✗ They found an evidence.
✓ They found some evidence / a piece of evidence.
Evidence is uncountable.
✗ He was accused, so he went to prison.
✓ He was convicted, so he went to prison.
Accused = suspected; convicted = proven guilty.
✗ Punishments should deterrent people.
✓ Punishments should deter people. / act as a deterrent.
Deter is the verb; deterrent is the noun.
✗ DE-ter-rent · le-NI-ent · RE-offend
✓ de-TER-rent · LEE-ni-ent · re-of-FEND
Word stress changes how fluent you sound.

Quick questions

Is your neighbourhood generally safe, in your opinion?

band 6

Yes, I think my neighbourhood is quite safe. There is some petty crime, like pickpocketing in the bazaar, but I usually feel safe walking at night.

band 7

On the whole, yes. It’s a quiet residential area, so serious crime is rare. The main problem is petty crime — a neighbour had his phone snatched last year — but the crime rate is low compared to the city centre.

band 8

Generally speaking, yes — though “safe” is relative. Violent crime is almost unheard of, but petty theft is a different story; phone-snatching by motorbike has become a bit of a local plague. So I feel safe, just not careless.

What would you do if you saw someone committing a petty crime?

Tip · It’s hypothetical — use would: “I’d probably…”, “I wouldn’t…”
band 6

I would probably call the police. I wouldn’t try to stop the person myself, because it could be dangerous.

band 7

I’d probably try to be a good witness rather than a hero — notice what the person looked like and report it to the police. Stepping in myself could easily make things worse.

band 8

Honestly, I’d like to think I’d intervene, but realistically I’d hold back and be a useful witness — get a description, maybe a photo, and report it straight away. A stolen wallet isn’t worth getting hurt for.

PART 2 · model
band 7

The crime I’d like to describe is a phone robbery that happened to a close friend’s sister last winter, on Vali-Asr Street in Tehran — one of the busiest streets in the city, which made it even more shocking.

It was early evening and she was walking home while chatting on her phone. All of a sudden, a motorbike with two men on it pulled up beside her, and the passenger snatched the phone right out of her hand. The whole thing took about three seconds. She wasn’t physically hurt, but she was badly shaken.

I heard about it that same night, when my friend called me, still quite upset. The next morning they reported it to the police, who were sympathetic but honest: this kind of petty crime is so widespread that unless there’s clear CCTV footage, the chances of catching the culprits are slim.

As for how it affected people, it changed our habits more than anything. My friend’s sister stopped using her phone on the street altogether, and I became much more aware of my surroundings. It also made me wonder whether stronger policing would act as a deterrent, or whether the real problem is young people with no jobs turning to crime.

CASE FILE #01 · CRIMESTUDY SQUAD
PART 2 · model
band 8

I’d like to talk about something that happened to a friend’s sister last winter — a motorbike phone-snatching on Vali-Asr Street. It’s a fairly common crime in Tehran, but hearing it from someone close made it feel very different from reading about it.

It happened at around six in the evening, right in the rush hour. She was walking home, phone pressed to her ear, when a motorbike mounted the pavement behind her. Before she’d even registered the noise, the passenger had snatched the phone and they’d disappeared into the traffic. Nobody around her had time to react.

I found out that night, when my friend rang me, half furious and half relieved that her sister hadn’t been dragged along the ground, which apparently happens. They filed a report the next day, and the officer was candid: unless there’s CCTV footage of the number plate, cases like this rarely go anywhere — most snatchers simply get away with it.

What struck me most was the impact. Materially, it was just a phone. But it took her weeks to feel comfortable on that street again, and it quietly rewired the rest of us — none of my friends takes a call on the pavement now. And it left me with a question I still can’t settle: whether these crimes call for being tougher on crime, or whether the answer lies in the root causes — unemployment, and young men with very few options turning to crime.

CASE FILE #01 · CRIMESTUDY SQUAD
PART 3

Do you think strict punishments act as a deterrent to serious crimes like murder or drug trafficking? Why or why not?

band 6

I think strict punishments help a little, but not completely. Some people are afraid of going to prison, so they don’t commit crimes. But people who commit very serious crimes often don’t think about the punishment.

band 7

To some extent, yes. Harsh sentences probably act as a deterrent for people who plan carefully, like drug traffickers, because they calculate the risk. But many murders happen in a moment of anger, and in that situation nobody thinks about the law. So I’d say the chance of getting caught matters more than how severe the punishment is.

band 8

It’s not black and white. For calculated crimes like drug trafficking, the threat of the full weight of the law may well shift the risk calculation. But criminologists generally find that what really deters people is the likelihood of being caught, not the severity of the sentence. And crimes of passion are, almost by definition, not rational — so no punishment, however harsh, is likely to prevent them.

CASE FILE #01 · CRIMESTUDY SQUAD
PART 3

How can communities work together to reduce crime rates?

band 6

Neighbours can get to know each other and watch the street. They can also tell the police when they see something strange. Communities can also organise activities for young people.

band 7

I think the key is social connection. In areas where neighbours know each other, people notice strangers and report suspicious activity quickly. Local councils can also set up youth clubs and sports programmes, which keep teenagers busy and reduce the chance that they turn to crime.

band 8

Mostly by doing what the police can’t — being present. Neighbourhood watch schemes, well-lit streets and simply knowing who lives next door make a place far less attractive to criminals. Beyond that, investing in young people through mentoring, sport and apprenticeships tackles the root causes of crime, rather than just reacting to it.

CASE FILE #01 · CRIMESTUDY SQUAD
PART 3

Do you think lenient sentences for petty crimes encourage reoffending? Why?

band 6

Maybe sometimes. If the punishment is very small, some people may do the same crime again. But prison is not always a good idea for small crimes.

band 7

Not necessarily. You could argue that lenient sentences send the message that crime pays, but sending petty offenders to prison often makes things worse — they mix with serious criminals and are more likely to reoffend when they’re released. Community service can be a better option.

band 8

I’d actually argue the opposite. Being soft on crime isn’t the issue so much as being smart about it. Prison is often a school for crime — a young shoplifter can come out a hardened criminal. Non-custodial sentences keep people connected to work and family, which is exactly what makes them less likely to reoffend.

CASE FILE #01 · CRIMESTUDY SQUAD
PART 3

How effective do you think technology is in preventing or solving crimes?

band 6

I think it is very helpful. CCTV cameras help the police find criminals, and people are afraid to commit crimes when there are cameras.

band 7

It’s become extremely effective, especially for solving crimes. CCTV footage, digital fingerprints and phone data make it much easier to identify suspects, and cameras can also act as a deterrent. However, technology has also created new crimes, like identity theft and online scams.

band 8

It’s a double-edged sword. On the one hand, CCTV, facial recognition and digital forensics have transformed investigations — it’s far harder to get away with a street crime than it was twenty years ago. On the other hand, the same technology has opened up entirely new territory, and cybercrime tends to stay one step ahead of law enforcement. There’s also a privacy cost we don’t discuss enough.

PART 3

How do you think crimes in the future will differ from those today?

band 6

I think there will be more crime on the internet and less crime in the street. For example, people will steal money from bank accounts, not from pockets.

band 7

I expect cybercrime to keep growing while traditional street crime falls. As more of our lives move online, criminals will focus on identity theft, scams and hacking. AI might also make fraud more convincing, for example with fake voices.

band 8

I suspect crime will become increasingly invisible. The pickpocket is being replaced by the scammer who never leaves his bedroom — and with AI-generated voices, fraud is becoming disturbingly persuasive. The irony is that as our streets get safer, our digital lives may become the most dangerous neighbourhood we have.

PART 3

Do you believe criminals who serve time can successfully reintegrate back into society? Why or why not?

band 6

Yes, I think they can, but it is difficult. Many companies don’t want to hire someone with a criminal record. They need help to find a job.

band 7

Yes, as long as they get the right support. The biggest obstacle is a criminal record, which makes it very hard to find work. Without a job, people are much more likely to reoffend, so training and support after release are essential for them to reintegrate into society.

band 8

I believe many can, but the system often sets them up to fail. Someone leaves prison wanting to go straight, then discovers that a criminal record closes almost every door. If we want fewer repeat offenders, we have to offer a realistic path back — education inside prison, employers willing to give second chances — otherwise we’re just recycling people back into crime.

CASE FILE #01 · CRIMESTUDY SQUAD
PART 3

Should young offenders be sent to prison?

band 6

I don’t think so. Young people can make mistakes, and prison can make them worse. Community service is a better punishment for them.

band 7

In most cases, no. Teenagers who commit a crime have often been led astray by older friends. Prison exposes them to serious criminals, so they’re likely to reoffend. A non-custodial sentence, like community service, teaches responsibility without destroying their future.

band 8

Only as a last resort. A young offender needs to understand that they’ve slipped up, but locking them up risks turning a mistake into a career — they come out with new contacts and a criminal record. Short of serious violence, community service or probation seems far more likely to help them keep their nose clean in the long run.

Is the crime rate lower nowadays than in the past?

band 6

I think some crimes are lower, like burglary, because houses have better security. But crime on the internet is higher now.

band 7

It depends on which crimes we’re talking about. Property crimes like burglary have fallen in many cities because of better home security and CCTV. But cybercrime has grown enormously, so overall it’s hard to say that crime is lower.

band 8

I’m in two minds about it. Statistically, many traditional crimes such as burglary and car theft have dropped sharply. But I’m not sure the numbers tell the whole story — online fraud is massively under-reported, so crime may not have fallen so much as moved somewhere harder to measure.
